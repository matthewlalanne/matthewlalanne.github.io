Where it stands

It started as Outlast, a private Survivor app for my own leagues. In five weeks it became Reality Check, an app where anyone can create or join a league for more than one show. Today one Amazing Race league plays in Reality Check, and my three Survivor leagues play in both apps through a two-way sync. It's in beta testing now, and it goes to the App Store after the Survivor 51 finale.

The problem

Most leagues run on a spreadsheet and a group chat. The commissioner types in every result, chases people for their weekly pick and settles arguments about deadlines. Nothing keeps a pick hidden until it locks, and nothing explains a score.

Outlast fixed that for my own leagues, but it was built only for us: one season record, our league names hard-coded, one shared password. Fine for a few dozen friends, wrong for anyone else. So I wrote down everything that had to change before a stranger could use it. That list became Reality Check.

How I work with AI

I designed first, and Claude built what I designed

I don't write code. I design it, review what comes back, and test it on a real phone. The loop looked like this.

1I designed the screen first

Rules before code. STYLE.md holds spacing, type scale, colour and "one job per screen". IA-PLAN.md cut six tabs to five and set the order of work.

2Claude built it

Then I reviewed what came back against the design.

3I reviewed against the design

In the simulator with fake data, then on my own phone.

4I sent it back until it matched

Then it shipped as an update, so the league had it the same day and I found out fast if I'd guessed wrong.

Small things took real rounds. The recap box in the episode editor went through seven versions in one day, from "plain text until tapped" to its own full-screen editor, because each one still had a keyboard or cursor problem on my phone. Chat took three tries before it reliably opened at the newest message. That's the cost of not writing the code yourself: you find out on the device, and you send it back. I'd rather ship a small fix the same night than sit on a build for a week.

The loop also needed rules for the AI, and the two that matter most came from mistakes. Once, an update reported success and reached nobody, because a config change had quietly changed the build fingerprint. Now every update is checked against the live build first. Claude also once claimed my phone build was broken when it wasn't. So the project rules now say: check before you say it, and say "I haven't checked" when you haven't.

I also had Claude build a fake mode, so I could poke at any screen with believable invented data and the database switched off. Nothing I tested could touch a real league, which matters when your testers are also your friends.

Beta testing with my leagues

My testers don't file bug reports. They text me.

Four friend leagues play every week, and they are the beta testers. When someone asked a question in the group chat, I treated it as a design bug: change the screen, ship it the same night, and watch whether the question came back. These are the problems that shaped the app most.

"Which episode am I picking for?"

What testers said: people kept asking which episode a pick was for, and the who-goes-home list was a long wall of cast photos.

Before: a Predictions tab mixing this week, the season and past weeks, with the cast as a grid of photos.

Now: one card per question. It names the episode, the air time and when picks lock. Each question opens the cast as a short list, faces included. Nothing saves until you tap Lock in, and the screen says so.

Why: a pick you aren’t sure was saved is worse than no pick. Naming the episode answers the question before anyone asks it.

"Where did my pick go?"

What testers said: after an episode, people couldn’t remember who they had picked, and asked where it went.

Before: last week’s picks disappeared as soon as the next episode opened.

Now: an Episodes tab. Each episode gets one page: who went home, what happened, the recap, then everyone’s calls and points.

Why: people open the app right after watching, so the story comes before the scores. The push that announces a recap never names who went home.

Recaps are written and checked by hand each week, which doesn’t scale on its own.

Notifications that never cleared

What testers said: the badge stayed after reading, and tapping a chat alert opened a different league. With several leagues, nobody could tell where the new thing was.

Now: every alert names its league. Tapping it switches to that league and opens the exact chat or episode. Reminders clear once seen, and the switcher shows why a league has a dot: pick due, new chat, results in.

Why: this caused real confusion every week, so it went first in the plan. Notifications are set per league, because you might want every message from one league and none from another.

Scores people argued about

What testers said: someone picked a castaway who quit that episode, and the app marked it wrong. Their point: they called who would leave, and that person left. Two players also saw different lock times for the same episode.

Now: an exit has a type (voted out, quit, medical), and on Survivor any exit counts as a correct call. Picks lock one hour before air, in Eastern time, for everyone, and the screen names the time. Every profile shows each week’s calls with the result.

Why: a score you can’t explain starts an argument in the group chat.

Home was busy. Now the next thing to do comes first

Before: a big countdown, a standings and recaps toggle, tribe banners, and every team opened up. I called it busy and confusing myself.

Now: Home opens on this week’s episode and what you still owe: your calls, your starters, your season picks. Standings are still on Home, right below, folded.

Why: every screen follows one rule: the next thing to do sits at the top.

Points leagues, so nobody is out after week two

Before: last one standing only: draft one to three cast members, and whoever holds the winner wins.

Now: a second style. You draft six, start three each week, and earn points for what they do, plus points for weekly calls. If one of yours goes home, you play on with five.

Why: the idea came from fantasy football and another league I’m in. With only two picks, losing both in the first few episodes means sitting out the fun for the whole season.

Content

Predictions, picks, calls: naming took more rounds than any screen

In a game, the words are the rules. A word that means two things shows up as an argument in the group chat.

1Predictions

The tab held this week and the season winner on one page. People couldn't tell which was which, so I split them into This episode and Season.

2Picks, then Episodes

The tab became Picks, then folded into Episodes, because everyone's picks belong to an episode.

3One word, two jobs

With points leagues, "pick" meant drafting someone and guessing a result. So: your Team, your weekly Starters, weekly Calls and Season picks.

4Small words too

"3 of 6 picked" sounded like only one person could be picked, so it says "3 of 6 have picked". Who went home says "Drafted by", not "Picked by".

Six tabs became five

Before: Home, Cast, Predictions, Games, Messages and Settings.

Now: Home, Episodes, Cast, Chat and League. Games moved to a row on Home, because they are played now and then, not every day. Settings became League, because people play in more than one league and want different settings in each.

Why: Apple suggests three to five tabs, and every extra one is a place someone has to remember.

I got one wrong first: I put Games on the Cast tab, and nobody could find it. It moved back to Home.

Creating a league

Five short steps, and the show does the typing

A commissioner picks a show, so the cast, schedule and results load on their own. Nobody types in a roster.

Games

Something to open between episodes

Two games, each with a league leaderboard. Testers told me early on that they loved them, so they got a real home and their own photos.

1Open Games

Scroll to the bottom of Home and tap Games.

2Use your own photo

On the Picture puzzle card, tap Use your own photo, then pick and crop it.

3Pick a size

3 by 3, 4 by 4 or 5 by 5, then Begin. The photo becomes the league's puzzle.

4Play memory match

Match the cast's photos. It unlocks once the league has six cast photos, and the screen says why.

Look and feel

Chat like Messages, and a sunset instead of a black screen

Chat works like Messages

People already know Messages, so league chat copies it: replies, tapbacks, and Read by under your last message only. Testers asked for GIFs, so GIFs came early.

Rebuilding Messages takes many small rounds. Chat took three tries before it reliably opened at the newest message.

Dark mode is a sunset, and it passes contrast

The palette is called Alpenglow, after the rose light on snow just after sunset. In dark mode every screen sits on a dusk sky and the cards are see-through. The second accent is rose, because a cold blue next to the pink sky looked like another app.

I checked 154 text and background pairs across every theme, light and dark. All pass 4.5:1. On the dark screens the lowest text is 5.7:1. One light-mode hint text failed at 4.2:1 and was darkened to 4.9:1.

Text inside the see-through cards changes with what is behind it, and I haven’t checked every case yet.

No network photos, and the app has to look good without any

For a public app I can't ship the network's cast photos or bios. A league can upload its own, and with none, every cast member gets initials on their tribe colour. My leagues added their own photos, which is what these screens show.

Accessibility

The basics are in, and large text now fits

Two accessibility passes so far. Here is what's done and what isn't.

✓Done

  • Every tappable control is announced as a button to VoiceOver (81 controls got the role), and the icon-only back button and menu backdrops have names. 82 accessibility labels across 28 files.
  • Text scales with the phone's text size, capped at 1.6x so rows stay readable.
  • The Ember theme's accent was darkened to pass 4.5:1 contrast.
  • Small stepper buttons got a 44pt tap target.
  • The app checks the Reduce Motion setting.
  • At the largest text size the chat box, initials, tab labels and the Home pick row all fit.
  • 154 text and background pairs, across every theme in light and dark, pass 4.5:1 contrast.

✕Not done

  • I haven't tested a full flow with VoiceOver on a device yet.
  • I haven't checked contrast inside the see-through dark cards, which change with what's behind them.

Takeaways

What I've learned so far

Testers report confusion, not bugs

"Which episode is this?" was a layout problem. "Where did my pick go?" was a missing page. Every question in the group chat is a design bug.

Name things before building them

Predictions, picks and calls cost four renames. A word list on day one would have saved most of them.

Put the next action first

Home, the pick card and the episode page all got better the same way: what you have to do now goes at the top.

Shipping the same night changes testing

A fix reached phones before the next episode, so I could try an idea, hear back in a day and undo it. Games on the Cast tab lasted one evening.

Design for strangers early

A shared password and one season record were right for friends, and cost a rebuild to go public.

Write the style rules first

Before the rules existed, screens drifted, and I had to redesign Home, Cast, chat and the tab bar in one push.

Where it stands

The code is Claude's. The product, the design and every release decision are mine, and so are the mistakes. It's in beta now. I'm sharing it before the App Store release because four leagues already depend on it, and the decisions along the way are the point of the write-up.
