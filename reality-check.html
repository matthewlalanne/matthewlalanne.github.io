My first app: a fantasy league for reality TV that four friend leagues play every week

Reality Check runs a fantasy league around a reality show. You draft cast members, call who goes home each week, and settle it in the league chat. I designed every screen and made every product call. Claude wrote the code. Four friend leagues play it every week, and I'm still learning how to ship software. This is what that looks like so far.

Where it stands

It started as Outlast, a private Survivor app for my own leagues. In 34 days it became Reality Check, an app where anyone can create or join a league for more than one show. Today one Amazing Race league plays in Reality Check, and my three Survivor leagues play in both apps through a two-way sync. It goes to the App Store after the Survivor 51 finale.

The problem

Most leagues run on a spreadsheet and a group chat. The commissioner types in every result, chases people for their weekly pick and settles arguments about deadlines. Nothing keeps a pick hidden until it locks, and nothing explains a score.

Outlast fixed that for my own leagues, but it was built only for us: one season record, our league names hard-coded, one shared password. Fine for a few dozen friends, wrong for anyone else. On Sept 24 I wrote down everything that had to change before a stranger could use it. That list became Reality Check.

How I work with AI

I designed first, and Claude built what I designed

I don't write code. I describe what I want, review what comes back, and test it on a real phone. The loop looked like this.

1I designed the screen first

Rules before code. STYLE.md holds spacing, type scale, colour and "one job per screen". IA-PLAN.md cut six tabs to five and set the order of work.

2Claude wrote the code

I described what I wanted in product terms and reviewed what came back against the design.

3I reviewed against the design

In the simulator with fake data, then on my own phone.

4I sent it back until it matched

Then it shipped as an update, so the league had it the same day and I found out fast if I'd guessed wrong.

Small things took real rounds. The recap box in the episode editor went through seven versions in one day, from "plain text until tapped" to its own full-screen editor, because each one still had a keyboard or cursor problem on my phone. Chat took three tries before it reliably opened at the newest message. That's the honest cost of being new at this and not writing the code yourself: you find out on the device, and you send it back. I'd rather ship a small fix the same night than sit on a build for a week.

The loop also needed rules for the AI, and the two that matter most came from mistakes. Once, an update reported success and reached nobody, because a config change had quietly changed the build fingerprint. Now every update is checked against the live build first. Claude also once claimed my phone build was broken when it wasn't. So the project rules now say: check before you say it, and say "I haven't checked" when you haven't.

I also had Claude build a fake mode, so I could poke at any screen with believable invented data and the database switched off. Nothing I tested could touch a real league, which matters when your testers are also your friends.

Testing with my league

My testers want to win, so they don't hold back

Forty players across three Survivor leagues and one Amazing Race league use the apps every week, and they are the test group. Here is what they ran into and what changed.

Sign-in codes never reached my friends.

The first email service only delivered to its own account owner, so nobody could sign in. Codes now come from my own Gmail, and the code screen tells you to check spam.

They asked for reminders and better commissioner tools.

Reopening a pick for a late player once took a code change on an episode night. A push now goes out 12 hours before each episode, and commissioners can add, rename or remove players, change the draft time and pick for whoever is on the clock, all from the app.

Notifications piled up, and with several leagues it wasn't clear where a tap would go.

Pick reminders and draft alerts now clear once you've seen them, and the league switcher shows a dot where something is new.

They wanted more to play for than "last one standing".

Points scoring was on their list, so on Oct 21 I added points leagues.

The season threw cases a spreadsheet never had to answer.

What happens when someone quits? Does a call on a quit count? How do you break a tie? Exits now have a type, like voted, quit or medical, and standings ties go to whoever's people lasted longer.

Decisions

Twelve design decisions, each with a cost

The weekly pick shrank to one card per question

The pick screen used to be a long grid of cast photos. Now each question is one card with a dropdown that opens a sheet. Nothing saves until you tap Lock in picks, and the screen says Not saved yet until you do. The host is an option on the title question, because the host sometimes says it.

You lose the faces at a glance, but the whole pick fits on one screen.

Home became a to-do list, not a scoreboard

Home opens on this week's episode with what you still owe: your calls, your starters, your season picks. Standings come below. In a last-one-standing league the same layout shows 1 left instead of points.

Standings, the fun part, sit below the fold.

Standings fold by default and show faces

Each row shows a rank, a name, a score and the player's picks as faces. Faded faces are out. Tap to open a row and see each pick's points and why someone left. I dropped Expand all because short lists read faster.

It costs one tap to see detail.

Every score can be traced back

A player's profile shows their team, who they started each week and every call with its result. Each episode page ends with Points this episode. I built this because a score you can't explain starts an argument in the group chat. Reviewing these screens for this write-up, I caught two misaligned rows and fixed both the same day, so every call row now has the same shape.

A long profile page.

Every episode gets its own page, and the recap comes first

Each episode has one page: who went home, what happened, the recap, everyone's calls, then points. People open it right after watching, so the story comes before the scores. Long recaps fold behind Show more, which keeps everyone's calls one scroll away. The push that announces a recap never names who went home.

Recaps are written for each episode and checked before they go up, which is weekly work that doesn't scale on its own.

Points leagues: draft six, start three, no swaps

Inspired by fantasy football. You draft six and start three each week. No swaps and no waivers: if someone goes home they leave your team, and if fewer than three are left, they all start. The Cast tab marks who is on your team and shows season points.

More options would add strategy, but every option is one more thing to explain in the group chat.

Chat works like Messages

People already know Messages, so league chat copies it: replies, tapbacks, and Read by 3 under your last message only. The league switcher stays in the header, because people already understood it there.

This is a rebuild of Messages in JavaScript. Oct 8 alone had about seven chat commits on details like tapbacks.

Dark mode is a sunset, not a black screen

The palette is called Alpenglow, after the rose and purple light on snow just after sunset. In light mode the screens sit on that glow. In dark mode I didn't want plain black, so every screen sits on a dusk sky and the cards are see-through. On Oct 28 I changed the second accent from blue to rose, because a cold blue next to the pink sky looked like it came from another app.

See-through cards change with whatever is behind them, so every dark screen needs its own contrast check.

Creating a league is five short steps, and the show does the typing

A commissioner picks a show from the list, so the cast, schedule and results load on their own. Then they choose how to play: Last one standing, marked Simplest, or Points. The screen says up front that this can't change after the draft, because changing it later would rescore everyone. Then they share a 6-letter code or a link, players sign in, and they're in. Nobody types in a roster.

The link version is built but not yet tested on a real phone, so the code is the fallback that always works.

Settings live with the league, because people are in more than one

The League tab starts with you, then this league, then commissioner tools, so players never wade through admin settings. Notifications are set per league, since you might want every chat message from one league and none from another. Message previews start off, and the screen says why: anyone who sees your lock screen can read the chat. Colours are per league too, and only you see your choice.

More settings screens to keep consistent every time a feature lands.

Games give people a reason to open the app between episodes

Two games, a picture puzzle and a memory match, each with a league leaderboard. Memory match stays locked until the league has added six real cast photos, because matching initials isn't much of a memory game, and the screen says why and what to do.

A league with no photos only gets the puzzle.

No network photos, and the app has to look good without any

For a public app I can't ship the network's cast photos or bios. A league can upload its own photos, and with none, every cast member gets initials on their tribe colour. Every screen here uses that empty default. It looks plainer than real faces, but it's the default most leagues will see.

Accessibility

The basics are in, and large text still breaks

I did an accessibility pass on Oct 21. Here is what's done and what isn't.

✓Done

  • Every tappable control is announced as a button to VoiceOver (81 controls got the role), and the icon-only back button and menu backdrops have names. 82 accessibility labels across 28 files.
  • Text scales with the phone's text size, capped at 1.6x so rows stay readable.
  • The Ember theme's accent was darkened to pass 4.5:1 contrast.
  • Small stepper buttons got a 44pt tap target.
  • The app checks the Reduce Motion setting.

✕Not done

  • At a large accessibility text size the chat message box clips its placeholder, initials spill out of their circles, tab bar labels don't grow, and the Home pick row cuts off at "Not picked...".
  • I haven't tested a full flow with VoiceOver on a device yet.
  • I haven't checked contrast across every league theme.

Takeaways

Things I learned the hard way, and would do differently

Design for strangers from day one

The shared password and single season record were right for a private app. Turning them into accounts and per-league data later meant rebuilding the app in a copy.

Check the name before building

Outlast turned out to be taken. A rename sounds small until it means a new bundle id, a new URL scheme and a new app on everyone's phone.

Write the style rules first

I wrote STYLE.md two weeks in. Before that, screens drifted, and on Oct 8 I had to redesign Home, Cast, Bio, chat and the tab bar in one push.

Where I am with this

I'm a designer learning to ship, not a developer. The code is Claude's, the product decisions are mine, and the mistakes are mine too. I'm sharing it at this stage because the learning is the interesting part, and because four leagues are already using it whether it's finished or not.
