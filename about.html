About

I became a designer to make someone’s day easier.

  • Denver, CO
  • Product designer at SpectrumSince 2022
  • Cal Poly, San Luis ObispoBA Sociology, concentration in Psychology
  • General AssemblyUX Design Immersive

Before I was a product designer, I worked at a foundation in Denver that serves people with Down syndrome.

A few days a week a coworker sat next to me, and we worked through our projects together in Word and Excel. Those programs were never built with her in mind. The buttons were small and hard for her to click, the menus didn’t make much sense to her, and I still remember watching her hunt around a website for one button she never did find.

I helped however I could. We bumped up the font on her to-do list so she could read her schedule, and that worked fine, but making her text bigger was about all I could do. I couldn’t change the software itself, and I wanted to be someone who could.

I studied sociology and psychology at Cal Poly, so I already cared about why people do what they do. I also grew up taking photos, which meant I knew Photoshop well long before I opened a design tool. In 2021 I went through General Assembly’s UX program, where my team won the hackathon with a money management tool built to be accessible.

2022 to now

Four years at Spectrum

Agents didn’t trust our tool

I joined Spectrum in 2022 and spent my first two years designing internal tools, the kind thousands of agents keep open while a customer is on the phone.

The one that taught me the most was a tool for comparing our offers against a competitor’s. It worked the way it was supposed to, but in usability sessions I watched agents ignore it and pull up a calculator or the competitor’s own website.

They didn’t trust what our tool was telling them, and they weren’t wrong to.

Deals change all the time, and entering them on the admin side took so much effort that the data was usually a step behind.

I designed almost all of what replaced it. In an early sketching workshop the team picked my layout, and it became the basis for the whole product: you choose a competitor’s offer, and ours lines up right beside it, line by line.

We also gave agents a way to report a new offer as soon as a customer mentioned it, so the admin team could get it in quickly. When we tested the new version, agents found a competitor’s offer in 43 seconds, down from over two minutes.

Bringing a vendor’s platform in-house

  • Lead designerTwo of us designing it
  • Launched after a yearThe vendor was retired

After that I led the design of the platform leasing agents use to refer new apartment tenants to Spectrum. A vendor had been running it, and every gift card reward cost Spectrum a fee, so the company brought it in-house. There were two of us designing it and I was the lead, and after a year it launched and the vendor was retired.

The corporate website

In 2024 I moved over to Spectrum’s corporate website. I started with small components, then co-led the redesign of the Home and History pages and designed the component system the whole site is now being rebuilt on.

On the History page, the ask was for custom, one-off image treatments in the hero. I recommended staying inside the component system, because one-offs take more design, development and testing and get harder to keep up every time the content changes. The version that got approved was built from a component we already had.

When it came time to move the rest of the site over, I ordered the work by what people actually visit and not by who asked first, so About and Leadership went ahead of Awards.

I also put in writing that Awards needed a full redesign and not a reskin. I lost that one.

What I could do was mock up the new hero on the old pages, so About and Awards get the new look now and not whenever their turn comes. It means living with mismatched type on those pages for a while, and it’s on this year’s roadmap.

Skeptical of AI

I use AI every day, but I’ve read enough confident, wrong answers that I don’t take its word for anything, so my workflows have to show proof for each claim before I’ll use it.

Right now one of them is rewriting our authoring guidelines by looking at the live components on the site, taking screenshots as it goes so I can spot-check it, and listing every place the live site and our Figma file don’t match.

The first guide was a rush request for character counts on a component that was publishing that day. Now I’m building one for every component, right beside it in Figma.

Picky about files

I’m picky about my files. I name my layers, set each frame to hug, fill or fixed on purpose, and pull every color from a token, which is how I ended up on the project to organize our whole team’s file structure.

I also took Deque’s course through our accessibility team, and I do my own contrast and controls pass before anything goes to them.

What I build on my own

Reality Check, for my Survivor leagues

The board in Figma, and the app that replaced it.

My friends and family play in Survivor fantasy leagues. We draft in person and run our own challenges to decide who picks first, and for years I was the one who went home afterward and built the board to track it all. It lived in Figma, one component per contestant, updated by hand after every episode.

That held up until I was in four leagues at once. So I built Reality Check, the app we all play on now. I make every design call and build it with AI coding tools, and I use every build on my own phone before anyone else gets it.

Every Wednesday night my friends and family open it up, and I watch what they ask and where they get stuck so I can fix it before the next episode.

Fire & Ice, for my husband

AJ loves penguins, he loves dragons, and at any given moment there’s a decent chance he’s playing a game on his phone. I’ve watched him play a lot of games other people made, and at some point it occurred to me that I could make him one.

The game is called Fire & Ice, and it’s about a baby penguin named Pip who sleds down a mountain with a baby dragon named Ember breathing fire beside him. I worked the two of them out from concept art until they felt right, and the game has grown to 100 levels.

The name on my apps, Last Chair Studio, comes from the mountain, because skiing and snowboarding together is our favorite thing to do. The pink and purple light on the peaks in the game is alpenglow, and it’s there because purple is his favorite color. It’s in nearly everything I’ve designed since.

He loved it. It’s not as polished as the games with real funding and whole teams behind them, but he loves the artwork, the way each world changes, and that it was made for him. He still plays it on every long drive and every flight.
